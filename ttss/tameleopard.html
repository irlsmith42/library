October 1937

Nothing, not even the last 60 pages of the finest mystery novel can hold us in suspense like the last 60 miles of a long-awaited train journey, and it was because of this that Miss Gladys Walker beheld, in the seat across from her, the impossible sight of a crisp new copy of Dorothy L. Sayers’ latest novel, Busman’s Honeymoon, the newest installment in the adventures of Lord Peter Wimsey & Harriet Vane and presently the object of her most fervent desire, lying open and unattended in the lap of a stranger who was staring, instead, out the window.

When Gladys had boarded at Southampton, this fellow had been seated there, absorbed in his reading, and the train had been moving by the time she noticed the cover and let out an exclamation. Over the clatter of the train, he didn’t seem to hear it, nor did the two other, older passengers in their compartment—completely understandable, she allowed, that he should be so entranced—so she set about trying to formulate another approach, a ‘pass,’ to find out how he’d acquired it so quickly, so far from London, and then, if he seemed at all intelligent, to ask him how he liked it. He was a young fellow, a little older than herself, judging by his prototype of a mustache, and it had taken only a glance to categorize him as a returning Oxford student. Gladys Walker, of Shepshed, Charnwood took this train journey often, and spent her unoccupied moments emulating Lord Peter’s habits of observation. Most likely, she thought, he had come from Portsmouth, after coming over from France; she perceived something foreign still clinging to this young man: to his clothing, or perhaps to his manners. It would have perhaps been more accurate to say that his manners lacked something English—a certain rigid, defensive apathy beaten into English boys who are sent away to school. This fellow seemed perfectly at ease in his corner with his book, comfortably slouched, his long legs relaxed, and he had a friendly tone when he spoke to the railway staff/servants. The first time he stepped out of the compartment, she discovered that he was quite tall; as soon as he’d gone, Gladys wanted to snatch up the book from his seat, but there would be two witnesses, and she restrained this urge. Tall, she reflected (moodily settling for more observational training), without being gawky. His body was well proportioned. When he gets back, she thought, I must observe his face, and see whether it, too, is well-proportioned.

It was. During the next portion of their journey she entertained the notion of falling in love, of enchanting this tall, mildly handsome young stranger of discerning literary taste by revealing her own wit and charm. “How did you meet?” people would ask them at tea after the christening of their second child—these were the sorts of questions Gladys would have to answer some day, and she was determined have answers with narrative interest and originality. “We met on a train. We struck up a conversation about Busman’s Honeymoon—yes, the Lord Peter Wimsey novel. It had just come out, you see...” Yes, Gladys thought with satisfaction, that story would do very nicely; and a story about a honeymoon would provide narrative structure for their relationship (assuming that the novel was as finely plotted as its precursors, which Miss Walker could not allow herself to doubt).

But the pleasant emotions brought on by these imaginary future memories could only last a little while before they faded; and soon Gladys was overtaken by the only emotion answering to anything that was really happening to her. For she had not, in fact, struck up any conversation with the fellow; she was behaving as the same meek girl she’d always been, and hiding herself in her imagination like her grandmother always said—and once again, her shyness was costing her terribly, for she still did not possess the thing she wanted, which was not a tall, mildly handsome fiancé, but a copy of Busman’s Honeymoon by Dorothy L. Sayers.

Consumed with envy, she glared at his book.

It would have added considerable narrative interest to Miss Walker’s story to know the truth about her tall, mildly handsome stranger’s journey. Even if she had made so bold as to speak to him, any feminine charm would likely have been wasted on Jim Prideaux; he had yet to be beguiled by the feminine charms of any girl, and had thus far been subject only to the feminine charms of men—namely, the charms of Bill Haydon, his best friend, who he was to meet at the end of this journey, and with whom he’d had a great deal of sex. As their train drew closer to Oxford, Gladys detected signs of his anticipation, though she could not have guessed at its depths, running even deeper than her own yearnings because it was dug down from trenches carved by a consummated relationship. She could only watch with growing astonishment as his eyes drifted away from the book—with scarcely 50 pages left!—to gaze out the window at the entirely conventional countryside beyond. After a moment, he looked back down at the book; and then, after hardly a page more, out the window again; he leaned against the glass, craning his neck to see the track ahead... Gladys was affronted: He would spurn Harriet and Peter, the greatest intellectual romance of the 20th century? For what vacant reflections of his own—? His hands rested on the pages, half covering them—why, he’d given up! Astounded, Gladys Walker watched him stare out the window, check his watch, bounce his leg, and check his watch again, mile after mile—and all the while, neglecting the book, the book! The book she yearned most desperately to read—an arm’s length away—and she could not read it, because she was unable to snatch it from his ungrateful hands, and, worse—for this was her most crushing disappointment, in herself—unable even to ask him whether perhaps she might borrow it, even for just a few minutes. Soon, the conductor’s shout came for Oxford, and the young man was up and out of the compartment before the train had even begun to slow down. Miss Walker saw him off with a furious scowl. Before the train pulled out of Oxford Station, she saw him again on the platform, talking animatedly to a porter; then he turned briskly and walked away into a curtain of steam, his hands empty.

If he went straight to the hotel, Jim knew he would be early. He had consulted maps in advance, beside the train schedule and Bill’s telegrams, and knew how long it would take him to walk there; nonetheless, he stopped outside to study the station map of Oxford, and reacquainted himself with all the relevant landmarks—his rooms, his church, the parks, his other favorite places—and then his destination, the hotel—as though perhaps the laws governing directions and distances might be different here, and he might find that in fact, if he went to the hotel now, he would arrive there at just the right time.

After the family tragedy and the subsequent dissolution of his parents’ marriage, Jim Prideaux had grown up mostly abroad, and consequently he was still studying the social codes of his native England through patient trial-and-error. He didn’t know, for instance, whether it was a faux-pas to arrive early to meet a friend for a drink close to supper time, which entailed a rather involved change of uniform; nor did he know what bearing the possibility of sex had on such a matter. He was unafraid of discovering that such earliness was a faux-pas, and placidly accepted the risk of a reprimand, partly because he would learn from this, but mostly because of an overwhelming sense that he must be at this rendez-vous as soon as possible. Such an all-encompassing urge did not seem like a desire to Jim, of the much-lauded natural athletic instincts: it was an imperative.

It was a cool, gray autumn afternoon. Jim Prideaux, restless after a sedentary day of traveling, was unaccountably warm, especially in his face and neck, and felt none of the chill. But the sense of autumn—bringing to mind its progression, and eventual end, winter, the new year—made him uneasy. The Oxford from which he’d departed in June had been lush and green and drenched; the spring of his and Bill’s acquaintance had been a stormy one, and each meeting had been bracketed by an expedition into the monsoon, and accompanied by the sound of endless rain. This cool, dry Oxford was bright, blank, aloof; whereas in June, it had assailed him with rain and wind, it now seemed indifferent to his presence, suggesting that its existence was unconcerned with him, and even that it had its own impenetrable life without him. The reverse was not true. Young Prideaux’s mental life had at last found an anchor, and it was Oxford. These last three months, abroad, alone or with fragments of his scattered family, he’d experienced a dislocation of mind and body that he had never known before. Wherever he was, he would read the foreign affairs section of the newspaper, keeping up with the news of England; he read English books—Austen, Fielding, naval adventures, medieval histories, murder mysteries—whatever he could find; looking at the date, he would add up the days that remained; in front of maps or globes, he would pause to look for England, then his present location, wishing obscurely to see them rendered in some sort of congruence, to know at least that they were in the same category, related in the mind of the world. Now, with no demonstrable transition, this waiting period had ended, and his body was back in the place his mind had been occupying. Oxford had fallen from the pages, maps, thoughts in which he’d been holding it: here it was... And somehow, he felt more agitated. It didn’t even feel like he’d reached his destination. Though he looked all around, looking for things that were different than his memory, for that might explain the persistent dislocation; all he saw was as he remembered it; and its familiarity disappointed him, and he didn’t know why. For a place does not contain in itself the desire we feel, when away from it, to be back there; and in the throes of first love, most of us do not distinguish our passion for the object of our desire, and our passion for the feeling of love. And in the continuum of man's self-awareness, Jim Prideaux was not a stand-out: He was not a diarist, he had not had a close friendship before, and his emotional processing happened without conscious effort, during his frequent meditations in the form of high-intensity athletic exertion. He did not read love stories as attentively as Gladys Walker, and he did not consider himself imaginative; he was the self-sufficient adult child of separated parents, bright and intuitive, accustomed to easily understanding whatever was presented to him. There was a frantic sense of time running out, as quickly as the dry leaves scattering across the stones, and a sense of rejection, as if this place he had longed for was somehow giving him a cold shoulder. Halting at a busy street corner, it occurred to Jim this might be his fault: hadn’t he wished all summer that time would run faster? Now, perhaps, it was—and still, still, he wasn’t there! In a gap, he bounded across the street, and then on the opposite side, slowed to a walk again; it wasn’t proper to run in this clothing. But everyone around him seemed to be moving so slowly. Couldn’t they see that the sun was almost setting?

A church bell began to chime in the distance, marking the hour, and soon more bells joined in, coming from all directions. The familiar, disparate notes from the scattered churches filled out a dimension of Oxford that Jim had forgotten: sound. It oriented him, and it soothed him. With a sense of pleasure and a touch less anxiety, he walked on.

At last, the hotel appeared. Jim ran across the bustling street, dashed up the stone steps; the doorman hardly had time to open before he was over the threshold, in the warm, dark vestibule, pulling off his hat. He looked up at the lobby clock—28 minutes still to go—nonetheless, as someone said something unintelligible about his coat, he looked in, through the high, gilded arch that opened into the hotel lounge—the place where, in half an hour, he would be. And so it was with a strike of pure, undefended emotion that he caught sight of him there, at the bar, already waiting for him.

Bill.

At the sound of the doors opening for Jim, he had turned, and their eyes met without delay. Like other guests in the lounge at this hour, he was in evening dress. He broke out in a grin and he raised a hand, mouthing hello, and Jim, his face now blotchy from the change in temperature, tried to wave back, but found his arm restrained—stuck inside the sleeve of his coat, which the cloakroom attendant was trying vainly to remove.

Bill stood to greet him: “Ahoy, the behemoth of Brittany! Fresh from the Channel—easy walk across it, I hope?” Beaming, he clapped his hands around Jim’s arms and spun him a quarter turn. “Look at you, towering over the populace... My! It’s good to see you.”

“It’s good to see you too, Bill.”

“I didn’t expect you for another half-hour at least! ...Did you come here straight from the station? You still smell like the train—! Smoke and wet newspaper, did you shovel the coal yourself?”

Anxiety, all theoretical until now, had stunned Jim like a tranquilizer. Bill—not the remembered person, not the silent voice that spoke one handwritten line at a time, but Bill, the genuine article, as real as the tables and chairs around him in the lounge of a fine old Oxford hotel.

“...It appears I’m under-dressed.”

“Nonsense. We’re only having a drink. And you look very fetching in your flannels.” He tweaked the little flower in Jim’s lapel, grinning with unrestrained delight, for none of Jim’s fear showed on his ruddy face. “After a channel passage and two trains, I would have stopped off at my rooms to freshen up, but that’s only owing to my incurable vanity.” He patted Jim’s arms again. “...My God, I forgot how tall you really are. Outrageous. Sit down, Hercules, and I’ll look you in the eye. What’ll you have? Drink?”

They sat. “Thanks. Yes. Er... Brandy.”

Bill signalled the bartender. Jim was uneasy; his hands felt too light, like there was something they were meant to be carrying, and he’d forgotten it someplace. On his face, a smile was breaking without his knowledge or control.

“Cold?”

“What?”

“What, what?” Bill mirrored Jim’s widening smile. “Grinning at me like that, you—Cheshire cat... Outside, man, is it cold outside? You wanted brandy. And besides—” Bill tapped Jim’s cheek. “You’re quite pink.”

Jim said, “Not so loudly.” Bill laughed. He pinched Jim’s cheek. “...You’re making it worse, I’m sure.”

“My specialty.” Bill’s fingertips brushed his upper lip. “And now what’s this? Mustache?”

“That’s the idea.”

“I like it.”

“Do you?”

“Yes—a brandy for my comrade here, thank you. Yes, I do. I think it’ll suit you.”

The swelling emotion inside of Jim was at last deflating, punctured by Bill’s easy, even eager touch, and the longed-for twang of his voice. Jim himself had no inspiration to reach out; he had an idea of wanting to, but no specific actions occurred to him. The sleek black and white silks of his evening dress had the same effect as a uniform, concealing the body by making it generic. Maybe, if Jim hadn’t been out of uniform, he could have made contact with Bill’s various surfaces, but whatever fluke had made them accessible to him from February to June had expired.

Bill looked away from Jim’s big, earnest smile and around the room, seeking conversational inspiration. “I saw Martins,” he said, “Today—he says hello. Cyril, too, he was tagging along with him—Obviously,” he added.

Jim snorted. “Cyril. Poor bastard.”

“Wrong tree,” Bill agreed. He turned to face the bar, so Jim could admire his profile. “He is a looker, though, Martins. And Cyril’s a romantic—though you wouldn’t guess it. He sends his love... Let me see, and who else... It was quite dead when I first got here. I think I spent a whole week in the library by myself, never a soul to speak to... I got your last letter forwarded, I think, the one you mentioned in your telegram? From Paris? Your uncle’s—?” Jim nodded. “How was the rest of your visit?”

“Fine—nothing to report. Read a good bit. Some of those books you told me about.”

He sat facing Bill, one elbow on the bar; the sound of his voice was wonderful, but their brief sentences and intermittent silences were painful to hear. He wanted Bill to talk, unhindered. He wanted to reply easily, without having to think.

“Oh, the er—Orwell?”

Jim nodded.

“What did you think of him?”

“I liked him.”

Briefly, they talked of Spain and the recent developments in the war. Outside, on his brisk walk, Jim's hands had grown cold, and now, in the heat of the firelit room, they itched horribly. As they spoke, he rubbed them on his thighs, scratching against the rough wool of his trouser legs. At the movement, Bill glanced down; self-conscious, Jim stopped itching. But seeing Bill’s gaze resting there, another feeling stirred in the space just vacated by his retreating anxiety: A flurry of desire, definite, and refreshingly concrete. Bill’s eyes moved across his splayed legs, then up the line of his tie, and Jim could almost feel it there—like a hand hovering almost close enough to touch.

Their eyes met.

Bill spoke again, automatically: “Jim, do you...” He trailed off. Unconsciously, Bill licked his lips. Then, noticing himself, he gave Jim another tiny smile.

He turned back to the bar.

“...It is good to see you.”

“Likewise,” Jim said.

Beneath their chairs, Bill gently kicked him.

“Did you miss me?” He said it teasingly, so Jim smiled.

“From time to time,” he said. Bill laughed, and turned away again. His foot swung back and hooked around Jim’s ankle.

“On the long, lonely nights...?”

“...Something like that.”

“What else did you get up to in France?”

“Nothing. Rode...”

“...Horses?”

Jim elbowed him. “...Horses, yes. Stella... one of my uncle’s horses, the one I wrote to you about. Bay, quarter. She’s one of my favorites. Very agreeable girl. We get along. She’s getting old now, but we still had a good time of it ...You?”

“Me? Have I been riding—? Only here and there...”

“No—I mean, your last...” Jim took a gulp of his brandy. “Your last few weeks of summer holidays. All well—?”

“I came back here early,” Bill said, looking off. “After Venice, and all that... Just got bored, really. There’s not really much to do at the ancestral home except for dodging beams of paternal disapprobation at the breakfast-table and invitations to go off and dine at Market Snodsbury...” Jim made a sympathetic sound. Bill shot a sideways glance at him; he detached his foot, and appropriated Jim’s brandy glass.

Jim said: “Oi...” Bill drank, and made a face. “Don’t like it?” Jim said— “Well, you see, it’s actually mine...” Bill didn’t give it back; he spun the glass in his hand, and drank from the same place Jim’s mouth had been. “Mm. Slightly better,” he said.

Without meeting Jim’s gaze, presently burning the hairs off the side of his head, Bill set it back down with an exaggerated smack of satisfaction. “Ahh—what?” He grinned at Jim, who looked to be smothering a number of urges. “Thirsty, are you?”

Jim snorted. He kicked him. “Piss off...”

Their conversation went on like this—the hesitant work of reacquaintance, tapping each wall to check for hollow panels, coming to doors and not opening them, noting each one. The nearest they came to frankness was when Jim asked after Bill’s paintings. He stopped looking at Jim and his tone became gloomy: yes, he had been painting, he said; “The studio opened in September. I’ve been going in and out... I’ve got one going that might turn out, and... maybe one other, but... mostly duds. Just a lot of muddy... mud.” He gazed into the mirror-backed shelves of bottles beyond the bar. Then he sighed. “I’m lacking inspiration, you might say. I’ve been reading about architecture, doing studies of buildings and things, trying to get ideas... I went out on the commons every night for a week to paint the steeple at sunset, but...” He was silent for a moment. “Perhaps autumn will be better for inspiration. Cycles of nature, time passing, you know... Dread, death, gloom... The burning, the ashes, the phoenix, the rebirth...”

Jim smiled a little. “To be sure.”

Bill nodded, taking a drink. “So—I’ll give it a few months longer, and if I come out with something serviceable, then I won’t have to top myself.” Jim snorted. “Perhaps I ought to paint some landscapes,” Bill said, as if this were a scandalous notion. “I’m rubbish at landscapes... But it’s only because they’re so bleeding dull. There’s nothing human in them...”

“Well... not if they’ve got buildings.”

But Bill’s face had grown somber. He rubbed his eyes. “...I dunno what I'm doing here... Sometimes I really think I should give it up. Go to Spain. Do something. I dunno.” He let out a deep, exasperated sigh. “...You don’t think about that, Jim?”

“...About going to fight in Spain? No. I’m not Spanish.”

“But about doing something, I mean. Like, what’s it all for—?”

Bill’s tone had lightened a little.

“Sure,” said Jim. “Sometimes. Not as much as you do, I’d think.”

“No...” Bill rubbed his forehead. “What do you think of, Jim?”

“What d’you mean? What do I think about the future?”

“I dunno. The future, the present... It feels as if I hardly ever know what you really think.”

Jim looked at him skeptically. “Does it? Then why do you tell me I’m a rubbish liar?”

“Mm—point taken,” Bill allowed. “You are a rubbish liar. So, to be more precise: when you’re lying, I know what you think for that moment. But the rest of the time, I mean... I mean what do you think about?”

“I dunno.” A frank answer occurred to Jim that made his face get hot. “Nothing special. The ordinary things.”

“Cricket? ...God? The state of the nation? The spectre of communism? Sodomy? Stalin, Hitler? Tomorrow’s dinner?”

Jim cracked a smile. “That mostly covers it.”

Bill smiled a little too, then let out a final sigh. Under the bar, he bumped his knee against Jim’s. “You wouldn’t want to come with me to the Chilterns for some plein-air painting sometime this term, would you? I'd give you dinner. I want to try a landscape and I’d be a lot less bored with you there...”

“Certainly.”

“Really? You want to? Carry my easel for me?”

“Love to.”

“Will you really—?” Bill beamed at him. He bumped his leg again. “Brilliant. When shall we go? This week-end?”

“Can’t do Saturday.”

“Cricket?”

Jim nodded. “Sunday?”

Bill mock-gasped. “And skip church? Jim Prideaux...”

Jim kicked his leg. “Tosser. I never said that...”

Once again, Bill hooked his ankle with his foot. Clumsily, he rubbed under Jim’s calf with the top of his shoe; his laces caught the trouser cuff, and made contact with Jim’s skin.

He caught Jim’s eye, and smiled. “Something wrong?”

Jim shook his head. “You’re unbelievable.” The grin wouldn’t go away; he was giddy with relief.

Bill suppressed a real grin, and let him go. “Good, then. Sunday it is. I’ll get Franklin to lend me his automobile on Saturday, and we’ll make our expedition. You haven't seen it, have you? It’s so swish, and he never drives it anywhere... I’ll pick you up, then? After the service?”

“11:30.”

“In front of the—?”

“Yes.”

“Splendid.” Bill clinked their glasses together. “Grand. There’s this charming little chapel out that way. I’ve got to show it to you. I only saw it once, and I think I remember where it was... It shouldn’t be too far out of our way...” And he went on, Jim listening in blissful silence as he sketched out all the things they could do together, now that they were back together.

Finally, Bill made a show of checking the hour. “My. The sun’s nearly set. Probably about time to get going—don’t you think?”

Jim said nothing. He could hear it in Bill’s tone.

“...No?”

“‘Going’ where?” Jim inquired.

“Well, speaking for myself, I’ve got a room upstairs for the night. But I can walk you back to your rooms, if you’d like, old chap—before it gets dark, you know—” He tried to hold back a laugh at Jim’s face. “...No? ...It’s a fine room,” he went on. “Very spacious. You’d probably like it. Big four-poster bed... Soft white sheets...”

“I’m sure."

“Plush window seat…”

“Mm—a window seat?”

“...Yes, a very nice one. With silk pillows. And so on...” Bill gestured airily, wrestling down his laughter. “So you’ll understand why I’d like to get back there soon—to get to sleep.”

“Of course. I understand,” said Jim, with no hint of levity.

“And I’m sure you’ve got to get home and, and—attend to matters.”

“Certainly.”

“Unpacking...”

“Yes.”

“...Jerking off...”

“Right.”

“Right...” Bill grinned. “So, then...”

“And the number?”

“My room number? Why do you ask?”

“Got to send something up to you.”

“Really? What’s that?”

“An assassin.”

Bill burst out laughing. He told Jim the number, signaled the bartender, and stood up, brushing Jim’s long legs where they stuck out from his chair. Smiling, he tweaked Jim’s boutonniere again, and left. Jim watched him walk away, his heart thumping in his stomach. But after a few moments, he became conscious of a sense of peace that had come over him here in this lounge, and was now settling like exhaustion after an arduous climb. He leaned both shoes on the crossbar of Bill’s empty chair and finished his drink; his mind buzzed with excitement, but in his body, he felt perfectly calm. More calm, it seemed, than he had felt for some time. The climb hadn't only been arduous: it had been long. Jim emptied the glass, set it down, and left for the lift.

There was a disbelief that enveloped evenings like this—and this one, especially—which did not seem to belong in Jim’s life as he had understood it before February. Jim never understood what it meant to miss someone; he would just think of them, and perhaps regret that he was not with them. That was what he had done all summer, as he made his solitary way from hotel to house to hotel—so incessant that he began to notice it, and even become mildly alarmed. It was Bill that he wished he could see. Bill was who he wished to talk to. Bill: His companion, his confidante, his first true friend; his seducer, his first sexual friendship, his only one to date; the only person he knew how to desire; the person whose conversation he desired above all others... For the first time, his mind was not with him, it was not where he was, in his body—the dislocation was especially intense in periods of inactivity, and he’d been obliged to go off running or riding, often for hours, even in the dead heat of midsummer. It was not only because of Bill; but Bill was the instigator, the trapdoor that opened in him, letting his mind tumble out of his body, fall to Oxford, to the past—to the spring, to the memories that were somehow of a greater value than anything else he possessed... His mind sought that feeling there, the feeling to which he had no present access—it strained towards it like a shaded plant hungry for sunlight, constantly uprooting itself from the present. It would have mortified him for Bill to know how often he had thought about him all summer, and how intensely, and where.

The habits of mind he’d formed between February and June: Every time they had separated, Bill would remain on his mind, never leaving again until he reappeared in front of Jim. And with every hour, day, and week of separation, the person and the friendship grew in his mind as his unexpressed thoughts and feelings accumulated and were polished by his ruminations into something vast and iridescent. It was impossible, his rational mind asserted, that he could actually be in intimate contact with something as rare and wonderful as this pearl that his emotional faculties polished and admired, day and night. It made no logical sense; and it was unsupported by evidence. ...And then the separation would end, and reality would present the evidence his rational mind had petulantly requested. Here he was, at last, again: in an old, beautiful hotel, walking down low-lit halls, the dusk glowing blue outside, to meet that very person. Alone. Uninterrupted. Unwatched.

When he knocked on the door, a voice inside said, “Come in." The lights hadn’t been lit, and the blue was fading from the sky beyond the half-curtained window; he could see that there was indeed an alcove window seat, a book abandoned on the cushions, and that was about all he could see. He was inside a dark, unexplored cavern. In the obscurity, he could make out no limits, only a dim raft of white, bisected by a thick post, like a mast; beneath it in the sheets lounged Bill’s shadowy form. He was no longer in evening dress. Jim pulled off his jacket and dropped it on a side chair. He went—loosening his tie—unlacing his shoes between his steps—to the foot of the bed, and when he had kicked his shoes aside, he leaned against the bedpost; not touching the bedclothes. Bill’s figure rose from the sheets: “Hello again,” said his voice, quietly; most of the archness was gone, and his voice was fainter, less studied; he only sounded this way in the dark. “Something I can do for you?” he murmured.

If there was any remaining idea that they might pass their time in some other way, it was now, finally, wiped away. Bill pulled himself up to meet him, the sheet slipped all the way down—he was naked—and their lips met, at last, in a kiss. Reacquaintance, as in conversation: dry and unbalanced, each uncertain of the other; Jim not breathing at first, then only through his nose, inhaling the longed-for scent of his friend, and alcohol. Jim thought of their last hour at the bar, face to face in public: they had not kissed there, of course—but it was a time when they might have, and the thought made Jim flush; it took him away from the kiss, into his mind. Then Bill straightened up taller, noisily exhaling, and reasserting his own reality. Jim reached round the post so his fingertips grazed the back of his upper arm. Bill inhaled—ticklish—and Jim stroked him again more firmly, and was relieved by the familiar feeling of him, and by his own recognition of it; he slid his hand around Bill’s back, cupped his shoulder blade, and opened his lips. In the gap, Bill’s tongue slipped into his mouth, touching his softly, and Jim sighed, tilting his head... And it all came rushing back, the feeling he had contracted so suddenly and irreversibly in February, so irreversibly that he’d thought of little else all summer, all those long, hot months apart—afraid of never having it again, having Bill—and all the accumulation of thought and feeling pent up in his mind for so long suddenly burst free, and crashed through his body in a dam-breaking wave, into every nerve and limb and inch of flesh—he threw his arms around Bill and clasped him tight, pulling his bare body against himself—but it wasn’t enough, not nearly. He was on the periphery, nowhere near the essence of the thing. He was hardly even touching him—he still had his clothes on... What Jim wanted now had almost nothing to do with his own body, and everything to do with Bill’s. And Bill was here, already bare, open to him. He pulled away, and they separated with a gasp. They were both panting in the dark. “Lie back,” said Jim, and blushed at the way his voice sounded.

Bill flopped back on the bed, his arms open, and Jim pulled his legs over the edge and knelt at the foot of the bed. Hastily, he unknotted his tie, got rid of it, unbuttoned his waistcoat, cast it aside, then shrugged his braces off. He flexed his neck and shoulders—free, finally. His mouth was dry.
