Operation Testify

Prologue

The truth is, nobody thought Jim Prideaux was right for that promotion. This move was not officially announced, and Jim was no great talker, so it was a mystery. It was early autumn when they last saw Jim—work alias Jim Ellis—pack his things for the train and like a boy getting sent back to school, and leave Headquarters for the southern outpost in Brixton. He was to take over what they called the 'Travel' department. This vexed them; it seemed a poor reward for a life of unswerving devotion. The news of his departure was spread by his aggrieved subordinates in a flurry of indignation—Balkans, the scrappy little department he'd fostered for the last decade—who staunchly defended their departed leader's honor against any imputation that this abrupt transfer could be in any way punitive.

Jim's name was not at home in the rumor mill, since his personal life, apart from his close friendship with Bill Haydon, was a spotless blank. But at the tail-end of 1970, the Windsor-Clive scandal was on its last legs and the Haydon affair hadn't yet begun, so it was a dull interbellum period in the theatre of workplace gossip. Jim's mysterious transfer to Travel was discussed at length in the cheery, windowless Research Department, where Iain Baron-Lloyd, presumptuous about his closeness to the centre of things, asserted that this removal to Brixton was most definitely not permanent: "A temporary measure, to be sure," he huffed, not knowing that Prideaux would never again lay eyes on the interior of these offices. "To be sure, my dears. No, no—a field agent of that caliber, with all that experience? No, you simply don't put a man like that behind a desk to rot… It's an interim role. Or else the Chief really has lost it."

Baron-Lloyd was the oldest hand in Research, but not its head. It was not as intimately involved with Circus operations as it had once been, and these days, his colleagues were mostly women. These women had none of his misapprehensions about the relative importance of their department.

"As though he'd have the faintest idea about the 'valuable experience' of some old field man," Eve Lyndon huffed later to her deskmate, Jennifer Chase. "He doesn't rub elbows with any more active agents than you or I."

"He's only trying to impress us," Chase replied, typing with unwavering precision. "He's probably repeating what he heard from some other windbag at his club." She forgot its name. It was one of the smaller, newer ones, with a vague bouquet of military/intelligence about its membership, which, needless to say, included no women. Lyndon and Chase, at least, were under no illusion that they understood these affairs, nor what it would mean to be a field man of "excellent caliber." To everyone in the windowless Research department, hired and trained after the war's end, a veteran field agent was a mythopoetic figure, a sort of herald angel who descends to Earth bearing file folders.

The same evening, in the Chariot—a club much older and more consequential than Baron-Lloyd's—two of the Circus's most experienced middle managers, Davids and Marleau, shared their customary brandy and news. Their tradition over this brandy was to discuss recent mishaps of management; all of which were, coincidentally, the fault of someone else, as they had been for the last 20 years.

Tonight, they were joined by McCaulay, a Circus man of the new generation, and, until recently, the Istanbul resident.

McCaulay was rebuilding his networks of information, now that he was posted permanently to London. It was he who brought up Jim Ellis's transfer, with the aim of discovering a rationale, or, failing that, the relative utility of these two hacks. 

"Chief's cracking up, I'd say," Davids offered conventionally, and Marleau nodded, yawning.

No one really believed that; it was just what one said.

McCaulay was disappointed by the uselessness of this explanation. "What makes them think Jim is… cut out for that?" McCaulay skirted the specifics of Travel, knowing his companions would find it a distasteful topic.

As if just waking up, Marleau frowned at Davids— "Say—he's splitting them up?" Davids frowned too. McCaulay knew that they meant Haydon and Prideaux; he'd heard more than enough stories about that pair since his trainee days.

"Why… separating him from Haydon? That's absurd." Davids was offended by this disruption of the status quo. McCaulay pointed out that the two men hadn't actually worked in conjunction for years now, but his elder colleagues scoffed. "You don't understand," they told him. That partnership was an institution; it was the institution. The mind and the hand. "You can't split them apart. It'll upset the balance."

Taken—and Jim always was, in the group consciousness—against Bill, with his 30-year trail of public and private liaisons, Jim had been endowed with a magnificent, mythological stillness. Bill was colorful, slippery, a performer; Jim was to them a solid mass which wore no mask. He was just what he appeared: an exemplary fieldman of the strong and silent school. He stood perfectly still until called to action, a rock beside his flighty friend's mad dance.

McCaulay endured a brief lecture on this topic. It contained nothing he hadn't heard before from other elder colleagues. He was unruffled. "Essential to the organization—yes, perhaps, when you joined up," he said. "I don't want to be rude, gentlemen, but the war ended 25 years ago. Our aims are different than they were when those two began; even from when I began. So our means must be, too."

Davids ignored him completely. He had not finished failing McCaulay's audition for friendship. "I'd wager Control's got something in the works… He's got Prideaux down there to work some angle. An internal investigation, I'll bet. A double-blind… And Haydon'll be in on it with them too. I'd put money on it." McCaulay smiled indulgently at this joke, glancing at Marleau—and was shocked to see no smile. In fact he nodded vaguely, like what Davids said might actually be true.

McCaulay bade them good night shortly thereafter, and laughed half the cab ride home. How they cherished the idea of these two spies even older than themselves running in the shadows… He pictured Davids and Marleau blundering their way through a double-blind in their own organization. Marleau couldn't even keep a one-night affair secret from his subordinates. McCaulay was still laughing when he got home to his wife.

Disparaging the chief, 'Control,' was a cherished tradition at all levels of the Circus; but just beneath Marleau and Davids, there existed a bitter senior underclass: old hands whose careers had stalled out on the lower floors, who now watched younger men like McCaulay get promoted over their heads to the fifth floor. No one cherished this tradition more than they did. The chief was an idiot; he had his favorites; he'd lost his touch a decade ago, he had syphilis eating his brain. To these men, critique of Control's decisions were not a matter of pride (no!)—they were bravely speaking up about dangerous institutional incompetence. Control's false moves endangered their organization—nay, nation… To say nothing of the loyal civil servants he left in the gutter.

You will find such people in any institution to which people devote their lives, by choice, coercion, or some opaque combination. When a colleague reaches this stage, it is incurable and terminal. They will go to their grave still sick with resentment. Darkly, they say, "This place has changed." What they mean is: "It betrayed me." And it has changed; all institutions must, to survive. People, too, must change to survive, but some forget this too young. These men calcified and ceased to grow; to see this might lead them to confront other more sobering truths. Braver men than these tremble at the thought of their own obsolescence. It was safer, far safer, to construct a sheltering narrative and huddle inside it, swapping tales of petty persecution.

But when the news of Jim's transfer to Travel reached them, even this bitter underclass, always quick to excoriate Control's latest personal decision, fell awkwardly silent. They sat around a conference table in a soundproof room, waiting for the boss to arrive.

He was an old hand; why shouldn't he come in? Too old for the field, certainly…

And Control was a fool; that was a given…

Yet no one could quite articulate what was wrong about it. Something was wrong. The topic bobbed on the surface, waiting for someone to take a bite; the water stilled.

"But Jim's a good man," someone said, finally. "And he's done his time." Murmurs of agreement.

"He doesn't belong on the outskirts," added someone else. "Corralling the bloody scalp-hunters." There was another uneasy murmur of agreement.

Here, at last, they hit on something, something which McCaulay, Baron-Lloyd, Davids Marleau, Chase, Lyndon, and all the rest tried to articulate; something Prideaux's subordinates were still loyally waiting to report to any review board if they were called to testify on his behalf; something everybody, old and young, from fifth floor to the basement shut-ins, all understood; something about assets being left to rot…

 It was plain he just wouldn't fit there. The "Travel" department was wrong for him. He was known to be a constientious strategist, never acting in the field without all the facts or a map with the missing territory clearly delineated and ready to be filled in. That was why his record had no blemishes or mishaps and why, as Haydon had used to say, his hair still had no gray. But Brixton was a brutal outfit. It was the iron fist of a civil service that pretended to itself to be completely civil, thank you; Travel was the exception. They kept them in a separate office, and nicknamed them scalp-hunters, and limited contact.

And Jim was tough as nails, yes; he had killed people, and not just in the war. And yes, Jim made the hard choice when it had to be made. But he had compassion. If you spoke to him for even a brief moment, you'd know it. Jim was the man who'd put down the beloved, rabid sheepdog in order to protect the flock. But he wouldn't pull the trigger until he had a clean shot, and he'd put the bullet between the eyes so it wouldn't feel any more pain.

This incompatibility was so obvious to even Jim's most distant acquaintance that for Control to overlook it was impossible; that this could be a failure of judgment did not bear consideration. Each time the thought crossed someone's mind, they quickly found a way to avoid that simple, ominous explanation, or to discard it as quickly as possible, and hide it behind a dozen other theories and inquiries, more intricate and picturesque. This was the first time this thought stirred in the institutional subconscious, and was ignored.

Children are still jealously hoarding all the knowledge they can find, and they are shocked by how long and far adults will work to preserve their own ignorance. The idea of choosing not to study a certain area of the map is unthinkable to them, because they still consider their map of the world an unfinished work period our map of the world is our greatest project. Not even my deepest hatred is as precious to me as that—my system of judgment, my sources of intelligence or information, my view across my nation. And even Control's bitterest enemies did not have the presence of mind to pick up and honestly examine this evidence that he might finally, truly, be stumbling.

Worse—more simply—to acknowledge any consequence of his age on his work could lead them to notice they were marching towards the same destination, at the same pace. In other words—nothing good could come of such an explanation. Best not to consider it.

For his part, Jim Prideaux was not aware of feeling anything particular about this unceremonious and unexplained expulsion from the heart of the service to which he devoted his life, and banishment to the important but repulsive little outpost of 'Travel,' down in Brixton, where it was kept away from headquarters like an infected toe. Prideaux was not a career-minded man, and it did not square with his self-image to be unhappy about a transfer of this kind, so he discarded the theory that he was unhappy about it, though he was not happy, and had not been since his promotion.

As months went by, despite how certain he was of his own indifference, the thought of his transfer of never left his mind. Not a feeling, but a thought: when will I go back? He didn't know why Control had picked him for this; Control hadn't even spoken to him about it. Prideaux was just as much in the dark as the rest of his colleagues. He wanted to know when he'd be sent back. But he had no right to ask, and no one to ask, since Bill didn't know.

Brixton was smaller and quieter than headquarters, with intermittent tempests of furious activity. All his new colleagues were strangers. He was, by a decade, the most experienced Circus man there, and the oldest. He inherited Ellen as his secretary, an impassive, implacable woman who quickly became a trusted right-hand; but she, like Jim, was not a talker. The office boys were ridiculously young and green; their operatives were rarely seen and had to be handled harshly.

Prideaux had failed an eye test shortly before his transfer; that was the explanation he gave when asked.

"You used to be a good shot, then, sir?” asked Chambers, one of the office boys whose only excitement on the job came through his in-tray. Prideaux vastly preferred their company to that of the operatives now under his command.

Head scalp-hunter Jim Ellis barked: "That's right. Used to. Now get back to work before you find out if the old man's still got it." 

"D'you need to be good at shooting? To be a field man?" Chambers made no move to obey. They had quickly learned that Ellis's threats were empty.

"Not if you do your job correctly."

A year went by with no word about reinstatement from Control, or anyone at headquarters. No word about how much longer he'd be down here. He didn't pester Bill, who was now on the fifth floor or in Washington, and therefore difficult to get a hold of.

Bill had been in Washington when the transfer first came in. Two weeks later, he'd found out, and called Jim from overseas in a huff. It was a disgrace, he scoffed—"And to replace you with bloody Bland. Damned insulting." On his return, he vowed, he'd inquire about Jim's speedy return. Nothing had come of these inquiries yet. Soon after that, all Jim heard of his friend was that one detested rumor.

In late winter, Prideaux went down to Oxford for a cricket match, something he had never done in the 30 years since he and Bill had left it; Then, halfway through [inning], when he thought about why he was there, he became embarrassed and left. As he rode the train back to London, he observed the countryside beyond the glass, dispassionately tallying every difference from his memories of their youth.

Accepting the permanence of this transfer was not easy, and he took to it like a test of manly strength. He had not prepared to accept that his life in the field was over, so soon, so sudden; but if it was, he had to. In Prideaux's complex and solitary mind, if something was difficult, it was a test to be overcome.

It was possible, he concluded sometime in the gloom of early spring, that his transfer was completely impersonal, and Control had sent him merely because the posts had become vacant and there was nobody better to fill it. His role was functional. And if the Circus was using him that way, then there would not probably ever be a reason to bring him back to headquarters before he retired.

Life in Brixton, he thought, is not so different. The commute was longer, and he no longer worked with people who knew well, or with Bill, and he oversaw more bloodshed than he ever had, excluding the war. He ran; he read; he played squash with the office boys, who were smart enough for the field but not tough enough for the scalp-hunters. (He vowed to push them to safety, headquarters, even if he couldn't extract himself from this dead-end department.) As the probability of return diminished, he thought of quitting the way one thinks of uprooting and moving to a tropical climate. His life, as far as he could see, was the same as it had been; Except that he woke up in the middle of the night sometimes, now, and his hair was going gray.

Prideaux felt that he really was on his way to acceptance when his two-year personnel evaluation arrived, and as he watched the examiner shuffle through his file, caught a glimpse of his transfer papers, filled out in a blue handwriting that looked very much like Bill Haydon's.  

Jim knew that the day was coming when everything would be set right and returned to the way it had been, before. The way they were supposed to be.

On a late October day in St. James, the change arrived.

He should never have lied. It was hot and late and this thought dogged Prideaux as he stalked down X street in the blinding light of the setting sun. He should never have lied, he was rubbish at it—and least of all to Max, with whom he'd shared a deep trust in the old days, and with 24 hours left till their departure for Czecho. Now he was annoyed with himself and pressed for time, and he had nobody to consult for advice, so he was searching for somewhere quiet to think, alone.

It was another long, hot evening in the long, drawn-out indian summer of 1972, and London, ignorant of the early snow already falling a few hundred miles to the east, simmered with barely controlled chaos under the setting sun. What Jim Prideaux wanted was a little garden or park, someplace with shade and green leaves; over his three decades with Cambridge circus, he had cached/mapped many such hideouts. But his search was frustrated by the seething streets. The heat was lifting everyone up off the sidewalks to drift along the streets in a haze, like strangers in a tropical city they no longer recognized. Prideaux was under no such daze. He lived with his feet squarely on the map; hhe always knew where he was and where he was bound.

On the street he sought, [name], traffic was stopped dead, and then two fire trucks lumbered by, wailing as they wove laboriously between irate motorists. The sirens grew more distant; he crossed to walk in the shade. Then the sirens grew louder again until he came upon the wreck, and was forced to turn around; Entire intersection cordoned off. Prideaux looped back one block and detoured around an old French school—boarded up, he discovered—slipped up an alley, and finally closed in on the gate of his intended sanctuary with the slowing pace of disappointed hope.

The garden was a sandstone-walled oasis of ivy in the heart of downtown. It was Bill who had first brought him here; years and years ago. For a time, they'd spent many an hour here after work, talking under the arbors until the sun had set. Shaded and green, and private, yes, but not quiet—the sirens were deafening. Jim laid a hand on the wrought iron gate, and peered inside. A curtain of ivy stirred; a man's legs emerged, re-crossed, and withdrew again. Gently, Jim pushed the gate. Locked anyway.

After another moment, Prideaux shoved off, and, lighting up fresh cigarette continued his search.

He couldn't go to Bill about this problem. He was out of town; most likely in the U.S., Jim thought, though that wasn't based on anything he heard at work; he never heard anything about Bill these days but that one detested rumor. Besides, Bill was strictly off-limits for this; Control had harped on that quite unnecessarily.

The library xyz, which had a lovely little courtyard, had changed its Thursday hours, so Prideaux doggedly cut down xyz towards the memorial garden at Saint James' Church, Piccadilly, where another disappointment awaited him.

The church was a lesser known Wren, an unpretentious brick and Portland stone, that had nearly perished in the blitz. Prideaux had been overseas for the whole of the war, and after the armistice, he'd been dismayed and unsettled to return and find his beloved country's heart just as wrecked as the rest of Europe. St. James' Church had been restored in the years that followed—a laborious, expensive process, undertaken because everybody in authority agreed that it was a worthwhile effort to rehabilitate the ruined thing; Prideaux had witnessed its resurrection in pieces on his commute, and so he harbored a special fondness for it, like it was a neighbor's baby all grown up.

Now, though, it seemed the blasted site adjoining the church had been sold for development; to his surprise, the heap of rubble and twisted metal that had lain beside St. James Church since the war had vanished, cleaned up or buried or hauled away, and the little lot was populated with men and machines, building something new. So the plain, serene little memorial garden behind St. James, his promised oasis, was overrun with the sounds of hammers and a cloud of gently floating dust.

Without expression, Prideaux took in this latest disappointment through the fence while he stood on the sidewalk, finishing his cigarette. The church had no signs prohibiting smoking, but he considered smoking on church grounds to be wrong. The shadows cut beams into the dying sunlight, each surface swirling with eddies of dust disturbed.

The church bell chimed the hour. Automatically, he checked his watch against it. It was later than he'd thought. He put an end to his indecision, put out his cigarette, set his watch, compromised on his wish for green leaves, and took refuge inside the church.

If you had asked Jim Prideaux whether he considered himself a religious man, he would have said no. This answer would not be a lie—he did not consider himself to be one—but it would not have been quite true, either. Though he no longer practised [Church of England] with the fervor of his childhood abroad, its basic framework still scaffolded his perspective. He expected an afterlife of some sort, for instance; He believed in a vengeful justice for those who sin; The Bible, he knew, was not the ultimate code of right and wrong, but he'd never stopped believing that such a code can and does exist, somewhere. He believed in a universe that that existed, objectively, and operated according to set rules that were correct and good—a plan, in other words, though he did not consciously think of it as such, nor of himself as a 'believer' of any sort of non-objective precept. But our oldest perspectives are the hardest for us to see, never mind to question; and Jim Prideaux's keen powers of analysis sometimes failed when directed at himself.

Inside St. James, it was dusk already. The door shut, dampening the outside noise, killing the flow of dust and light and warmth. Nothing further could be heard in the entryway as Prideaux entered, the way he went in and out of every building—silent as a breeze.

In a back corner pew in the quiet church, after an undetectable scan of the weekday worshippers, Prideaux sat, and pulled from his interior breast pocket an envelope.

In the quiet cage of the half-dark church, Prideaux called his thoughts to order.

The operation would be straightforward: into Czecho to meet with a source. He'd explained it over lunch and a map of that afternoon, drinking companionably with Max in the little yellow kitchen of a recently acquired scalp-hunters service flat. He still had the only key, but, infected by Control's paranoia, he'd arrived before Max to check the wires.

Like the old days, they conversed in Czech, Jim's hesitant from two years' disuse; but as their conversation wore on, and his contributions kept stopping short in front of closed doors, he wondered if this wasn't his first substantial conversation of any kind in a long while. Maybe since the stone steps.

In the old days in Czecho, Max served as Jim's legman numerous times, on much more involved and risky operations. They always liked each other, and in '68, they became indispensable to each other. Max was half a head shorter than Jim and twice as broad. Like Jim, he was a deposed aristocrat, the final link in a proud dynasty with nothing left to pass down to its last surviving son. But Max had his pride, still, if not his money; He dressed smartly, in fine clothes. Jim had told him this was foolish, and risky, and Max always told him good naturedly to go to hell. They were the same sort of man, at bottom; men who hate themselves will despise such a person, but Max and Jim were not that sort.

In the little yellow kitchen, Max had congratulated Jim on his promotion. “Good for you, Jim. Only, Bland is an idiot. Can you come back?”

He shrugged. "Love to."

He gave Max the explanation he'd adopted of late—his eyesight. Jim had the kind of "good health" that meant he never had to think about it. He was the sort of man who only goes to the doctor when something is seriously wrong, and nothing ever was, so he never went. Then one day, at the shooting range, the examiner recommended an eye exam. Sure enough, the staff doctor said, he was a bit farsighted. Jim was surprised and somewhat offended. He was not used to his body taking unsupervised action. They issued him a pair of reading glasses, which he refused to wear. He became more conscious of his eyes inside his skull, how much time his pupils(?) took to focus; in the bathroom mirror, he examined them, trying to see into the black apertures, but he could only see it shrink and grow, not what was inside, what was worn out, what was beyond repair.

"You know what they say. If I can't shoot, I can't work in the field."

"But you can shoot," Max said.

Jim shrugged with one shoulder. "Yes."

"So—what the hell?"

"I really don't know. That's my best guess."

Then Max had asked about the Haydon affair. "Is it true? I heard it from Toby, but Toby is full of shit."

Jim finished his drink and poured another. Sharply, he said: “Is that what Toby does down in Kenton, then? Gossip? I suspected as much."

They switched to business shortly after that.

Rule #1: Never go alone. Jim was the general's contact. Max's job was to watch his back.

Rule #2: Don't go to a second location without leverage. Control had that—enough to expose the general.

Rule #3: Always have an escape route. Max's van.

Rule #4 and #5 were axiomatic—Do not operate with a personal motive, and only obey orders from a superior that you trust.

Rule #6: High-tail at the first sign of trouble.

Rule #7: Don't blab. Jim had told no one but Max, and he trusted Max to do the same.

Once everything was ironed out, they would not make contact again until their rendezvous in Brno—except in one particular case.

Unless, for some reason, Jim called it all off. He showed Max a stamped, blank envelope, and said, if you get this in your mailbox on or before Friday, everything's off. Burn your tickets, go back to Kenton on Monday, none of it ever happened.

“Sure, Jim,” Max said.

The only reason he'd call it off was if something was wrong.

Now in the church, Jim stared fixedly at the envelope in the shadowy trench between the pews. Something was wrong. Something was wrong, or else he wouldn't be here, contemplating blowing off an assignment.

There's no sense avoiding it, he told himself. You've made a muddle of things—clean it up.

He should never have lied to Max. It had spoiled the whole thing, he could feel it.

It had been at the end, when Max was putting on his hat and slinging his coat over his arm. He'd asked casually, "Jim. If nobody else knows about this, will anybody come looking if we don't come back?"

The rector shuffled slowly past Jim and lit the first sconce. Jim closed his eyes.

In order to gauge whether this was a mission-destroying error, he needed to follow the thread of this bad feeling. If his conscience was unsettled, something was wrong. Jim had good instincts; Everyone said so. He acted on instinct because the mechanics of his mind works posed an open, in good working order. He acted based on knowledge, experience, and bias he was conscious of; Because of this, he lived honestly with himself. He wasn't like those hoods who have a girl in each city and tell himself he loves each one truly. He had no girls. He had no creaky floorboards in his conscience, his identity, or his worldview. He'd never entered into performative arrangements in the personal sphere. He lived a life on one level: he worked for the circus. he did cruel things in the name of his country, sometimes violent things. He did not then return home to someone and lie about it.

He'd known too many other men who had married, only to become laden with secrets and then separate, like his parents had. For what? He'd get nothing out of such an arrangement. Neither would she.

He could not talk to Bill about it at all. Control in the interminable brief, had put a humiliating fine point on that: “We trust no one with this. Do you hear me, Prideaux?" He never used work names. "That means no one. No bosom friends or old bedfellows—understand?"

"Obviously." Jim had bristled at the unprecedented implications upon his professionalism. When had he fallen that far and Control's esteem?

It wasn't until hours later, trapped in circling conversation beside the dying fire, when Control finally coughed it up—the last, undigested pellet—his “theory”—that Jim finally understood why Control wanted Bill, in particular, to be kept in the dark.

By then, all petty personal anxieties had been eclipsed by a deep dread about the man at the helm of their organization. Control was not well. This was not 'slowing down' from age—it was something far worse. And the longer Control's paranoid rambling went on, the deeper he led Jim into his delusion, the more the way back out receded into the dark, until Jim knew that Control was no longer capable of finding his way back out.

When Max's query struck too close to the source of their orders, then Jim lied.

“…Will anyone come looking for us?”

"Of course."

"They tell you that?"

"Of course."

A pause. “Who, then?"

"Come on, Max.”

Max hadn't believed him. And—worse—he had clapped him on the shoulder and let it drop, choosing to trust him instead.

He slapped the blank envelope against the heel of his hand. Control…

If Jim didn't trust his orders, the mission was over before it began.

Prideaux stood up—somewhat abruptly, earning himself a reproachful glance from his nearest neighbor. A verification of Control's credibility was what he wanted. He knew where to find that. Even if Bill had been available—and home—there was no need to ask him. He slipped out the back door, pausing a moment under the scallop shell. St. James, patron saint of pilgrims. A cool breeze rolled across the dusty courtyard. He tucked Max's envelope safely back inside his jacket, next to his heart, and set off in the direction of George Smiley's home in Chelsea.

Coming from a broken home, Jim had a perverse instinct to protect those in authority above himself. So he was, in a grim sort of way, relieved that Control had chosen him for this confidence, because it would enable Jim to shield him from humiliation. He would not run this story to personnel or the minister or even Bill; he'd consult with George, and gauge what to do next. The service which, since his recruitment at age 19, had taken the form of a sealed bank vault in his mind, now began to look like a bank vault with the guard asleep. If if thieves had already broken in and began spinning the dials, Jim didn't know. He couldn't see anymore.

Bill had to know already. Surely, Jim reasoned. He was at the heart of the action, and he was cleverer than Jim. He didn't need Jim to tip him off.

This thought reminded Jim of Control's theory, which he was not supposed to think of outside his memory clearinghouse, so he stepped over it like a ditch. Back to Bill. He couldn't see him. It was impossible. So why did he keep returning to this idea? In the hall outside his mental office there stood a thought waiting patiently for its turn to come in. He could see its outline through the frosted glass. It had knocked twice while he sat in the church.

Not now, he told it; Too late. if I couldn't see you in the church I certainly can't see you now.

It was possible, he allowed, that these thoughts about Bill had been dislodged by Max's offhand comment about the detested rumor. Members of the Brixton office had quickly learned not to bring it up in Jim's presence.

He missed Bill. It wasn't unusual in their life together, spending months at a time apart—nature of the job—so Jim had no qualms addressing this feeling. Yes, he missed him, and he wanted to see him.

Jim clapped him on the shoulder. Trying to instill confidence. "You too, Max. See you."

He walked the long stretch between Max and the Fiat, cold little clouds puffing out of his mouth, and as he walked past the desolate hayfield, his mind went blank, like a speaker on their way to the podium who, having rehearsed their speech countless times, can no longer remember a single word.

The driver's side door cracked open when Jim was ten paces away. "Are you all right?"

"Fine," replied a male voice in Czech, muffled behind the half-open door. "My doctor tells me I shouldn't drive more than three hours without resting." He heard the sound of a newspaper rustling. The speaker's voice had a Hungarian accent, and a contempt that made Jim take an instant, predictable dislike to him. He smelled garlic.

"Long journeys can be a strain on the heart," he affirmed.

The newspaper rustled shut, and the speaker leaned out of the shadow so Jim could see his face. He had hulking shoulders and a stupid Stalin mustache. He regarded Jim a moment and said, "You going to get in?"

The driver's door shut. Jim kept his hands loose at his sides, not in his pockets; just in case. The car turned on, and he heard the doors unlock. He pulled the handle. Locked. The driver was lighting up as the engine turned over. Jim rapped sharply on the rear window glass. The driver's side window rolled down an inch.

He kept checking his watch. Their progress up the winding mountain road was slow without headlights. He watched the clouds behind the trees. There was no moon, and he couldn't see a thing. The landscape was a dim blue haze of snow, disappearing and reappearing as dark shapes drifted past.

Jim observed that the track had been cleared of snow, then rutted by heavy tires before freezing again. He couldn't quite tell in the dark, but it looked like more than one heavy vehicle had been up this road since the snowfall, and he could not distinguish any returning tracks.

The driver was telling a story, he was dimly aware, a dirty story, which he pointedly ignored. Either an expression of nerves or of masculine aggression. Jim tried not to let it bother him. Anticipation drummed in his stomach, against the warm metal of the gun shoved inside his waistband. At last, he and Stevcek were drawing close; soon they'd come to the cabin, alone in the woods, waiting; in the middle of a sentence, the Magyar shifted gears and Jim's eyes snapped to his gloved hands. He killed the engine without another word, and they coasted down a short slope.

Jim didn't like that.

As the driver reached for the handbrake, Jim grabbed him by the ear and smashed his head against the window post. In another two seconds he had the driver's gun, snatched out of the holster under his armpit. They slid to a muffled stop in the muddy snow.

The Magyar swore and breathed heavily. Barrel pressed into his temple and his other hand pinning the man's wrist, Jim looked out the car windows. They had come to rest at the entrance to a little footpath, framed by a charmingly rotted arbor. Beyond it, the path led about thirty yards to a little wooden hut. It was smaller than what he'd pictured. No lights. No smoke. No sign of life.

"All right," Jim said, keeping the gun raised and transferring his grip to the front of the driver's coat, "Here's what I'd like for you to do, tovarish. You're going to put on my coat and my fur hat and take a walk for me." The driver started to say something. Jim pressed the gun barrel into his neck. "You're going to take it nice and slow, with your hands linked behind your back, straight down the center of the path. If—" He emphasized this point with the gun— "you fail to do either of those things, I will shoot you. Now, when you arrive, you'll go inside and explain to the General that his British contact is a paranoid old hand, indulging in an elementary precaution. Then, you'll come back out, walking nice and slow, and you can tell me whether or not the General is ready. Understand?"

He made him turn the engine back on, and execute a quarter turn to point the car down the path. "If there's any monkey business," he explained, "I'm going to turn on the headlights, and shoot you along the beam. Not once, but several times, and not in the legs. Clear?"

Sourly, the driver got out the passenger side door and slammed it shut. At Jim's direction he'd left the engine running. From the back, Jim leaned against the front seat and watched him walk. He ducked under the arbor, almost knocking off the fur hat. Then he went down the path in a slow and steady fashion, two pale hands behind his dark back. He was about ten meters from the hut when the lights came on.

Floodlights, bright enough to light a stadium, illuminated a space as vast and white as an arena. Dark shapes dove from the trees, hurtling towards the Magyar—one of them hit, sandbagging him, and the shooting began, startlingly near. The instant the lights turned on, Jim dove to the front seat and scrambled to get his feet on the pedals—he released the handbrake, put it in reverse, and hurtled back; from the windscreen he saw the scene still playing out: no one was shooting, though the shooting was deafening, and the men from the trees were now standing back, staring at the shape of the man on the ground in Jim's fur hat. He slammed on the brake and put it in drive, yanked the belt around himself, then hit the gas and flew at the hill. Another flash in his mirror—a camera. It seemed more theatrical than military—flares exploded in his mirrors, spotlights swiveled up, and the guns played like a frenzied drumline.

The Fiat made admirable speed up the steep, rutted track, and he crested the hill and dove back into the dark woods. The sounds of the guns and flares became muffled behind the earth. Damn it all, he thought, and turned the headlights on—he couldn't make it to safety if he drove into a ditch—from here it was all downhill, he knew, and a half hour wasn't far, not far at all. The Fiat sped around a switchback, and the noise faded behind him; the next switchback approached; he killed the headlights and slowed down, now that he was clear, better to be cautious than to be—

From the woods to his right came a deafening battery of machine gun fire—so close that the car flew off the road. The woods whirled into the sky. He saw a wheel fly past his windshield. Then the world beyond the windows disappeared into darkness as the car landed belly-up in the deep drifts of snow.

He unbuckled his seat belt and slid ungracefully onto the ceiling of the car. His neck and shoulders ached. He still had a chance, he reasoned, if he could take out the machine-gunner and escape into the dark; but the cold air coming in the broken window made his heart sink. He didn't have a coat anymore, he realized. Without a coat in the mountains at night, the chances of survival were far, far lower.

The machine gunner was the only adversary whose location he presently knew. He liked his chances with the Magyar's pistol; he could keep his own as backup. He crawled from the broken window on his elbows as quietly as possible, and pulled himself up the rim of the ditch, shielded by the car. He cocked the gun and held it at the ready, peering through the slit between the car and the snow. Cold water seeped into his clothes. Where was the bastard…

The next burst of machine gun fire came from not from there but from behind him. He was thrown against the car with shocking force. Two bullets in the back—shoulder—right side—he rolled. Face-up in the snow. Now he could see the lights dancing on the trees overhead, distant beams from the arena stage… no, headlights. Coming closer.

The sensation in his back was not pain. It was motion. His body was still vibrating from the shock; the force radiated from the impact point, and then when it reached his extremities, it started again. He'd never felt anything like it. He felt certain he should be dead, and wondered how his right arm was still attached at all, and whether it would be for much longer.

Shooting continued, as continuous and jubilant as fireworks. None of it aimed at him or the Fiat. Red lights; headlights; a klaxon. He began composing his report to Control in his head. Then, he would write, an ambulance came slowly up the path. The ambulance boys got out and just stood there, looking down at me bleeding in a snowy ditch...

The woods were blurry and fading. He could still hear; the other car stopped, and doors opened and slammed, and voices shouted, and there was a camera flash. They got me after all, he thought ruefully, and then wondered why he couldn’t understand what the officer was saying to the photographer. Then he recognized a word. It was in Russian.

I'm sorry, sir, he would say to Control, when he gave his report, back in the St. James flat; they finally heaved him up onto the stretcher and took him away from his car in his ditch.
